2.0 Introduction

Electrochemistry is the study of production of electricity from energy released during spontaneous chemical reactions and the use of electrical energy to bring about non-spontaneous chemical transformations.

2.1 Electrochemical Cells

▶ The device which converts the chemical energy liberated during the chemical reaction to electrical energy is called electrochemical cell. These are also called galvanic or voltaic cell e.g., Daniel cell.

2.2 Galvanic Cells

▶ In this device, the Gibbs energy of the spontaneous redox reaction is converted into electrical work which may be used for running a motor or other electrical gadgets like heater, fan, geyser, etc.

▶ An electrochemical cell consists of two compartments known as half cells. A half cell consists of a metal rod (called electrode) dipped into solution of its salt called electrolyte.

▶ The two half cells are connected by a metallic wire through a voltmeter and a key, and electrolyte of the two half cells, are connected internally through a salt bridge. Salt bridge completes the electrical circuit and maintains the electrical neutrality of electrolytes.

Fig. (a) Galvanic cell — Zn(s) | Zn²⁺(aq) || Cu²⁺(aq) | Cu(s)

Cell reaction: Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s)

When Eₑₓₜ = 1.1 V, electrons flow from Zn rod to Cu rod; hence current flows from Cu to Zn. Zn dissolves at anode and copper deposits at cathode.

2.1 — 2.2 Half-Cell, Anode, Cathode

▶ Anode : The half cell of a galvanic cell in which oxidation takes place is called anode. It has a negative potential with respect to the solution. Hence, denoted by (–) ve sign. It is written in the left side in the cell representation.

▶ Cathode : The half cell of galvanic cell in which reduction takes place is called cathode. It has positive potential with respect to the solution. Hence, denoted by (+) ve sign. It is written in the right side in the cell representation.

Electrode Potential and Standard Electrode Potential

▶ Electrode potential : The tendency of an electrode to get oxidised (i.e., lose electrons) or it is the tendency of an electrode to get reduced (i.e., gain electrons).

▶ Reduction potential : The potential difference set up between metal and its ions in the solution or it is the tendency of an electrode to get oxidised/reduced.

▶ According to IUPAC convention, standard reduction potential is called standard electrode potential.

▶ Cell potential : The potential difference between the two electrodes of a galvanic cell is called cell potential. It is different in electrodes and anode.

ΔE = E₍cathode₎ – E₍anode₎    (Eₘf)
Electromotive force : The cell potential when no current is drawn through the cell is called electromotive force (emf) of the cell.

For a cell reaction to be spontaneous, E₍cell₎ must be positive.

This is illustrated by the following example :

Cell reaction : Cu(s) + 2Ag⁺(aq) → Cu²⁺(aq) + 2Ag(s)    ... (i)
Cathode (reduction half reaction) : 2Ag⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ → 2Ag(s)    ... (ii)
Anode (oxidation half reaction) : Cu(s) → Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻    ... (iii)
It can be seen that the sum of (ii) and (iii) leads to overall reaction (i) in the cell and that silver electrode acts as a cathode and copper electrode acts as an anode.
Salt bridge provides cations and anions of almost same ionic conductance and removes the liquid junction potential which arises because of the difference in the rates of diffusion of cations and anions from more concentrated solution to less concentrated solution.

2.3 Measurement of Electrode Potential

▶ The absolute potential of a half cell cannot be determined. Only the difference between the two half cell potentials can be measured.

Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE)

▶ It is used as reference electrode whose electrode potential is taken as 0.00 V at 298 K. It is often constructed through 1 M H⁺ ions solution at 1 atm and 298 K. It consists of inert Pt electrode coated with platinum black.

For the cell, H⁺(aq) + e⁻ → ½ H₂(g) ; E° = 0.00 V

The half cell is represented as: Pt(s) | H₂(g) (1 bar) | H⁺(aq) (1 M)

The half cell whose potential is to be measured is made cathode. Standard hydrogen electrode is made anode and a galvanic cell is constructed.

▶ The electrode at which reduction occurs with respect to SHE has positive reduction potential, while the electrode at which oxidation occurs with respect to SHE has negative reduction potential.

e.g., (i) For the cell : Pt(s) | H₂(g) (1 bar) | H⁺(aq) (1 M) || Cu²⁺(aq) (1 M) | Cu(s)

E°cell = 0.34 V ∴ E°R = 0.34 V

Standard electrode potential of Cu²⁺/Cu is positive which indicates that Cu²⁺ is reduced more easily than H⁺ ion. i.e., hydrogen can reduce copper ions.

Note: The standard electrode potential is taken positive if the half cell is on the right side of SHE; and it is taken negative if the half cell is on the left side of SHE.

2.4 Conductance of Electrolytic Solutions (cont.)

▶ Resistance, R of a conductor is directly proportional to its length (l) and inversely proportional to area of cross-section (A).

R ∝ l/A or R = ρ l/A   where, ρ is called resistivity or specific resistance, having SI units Ω m.

The inverse of resistance, R is called conductance, G.

G = 1/R = 1/ρ · A/l = κ · A/l   and the SI unit of conductance is S or ohm⁻¹ (mho).
κ = 1/ρ = 1/R · l/A   where, κ is called conductivity or specific conductance, having SI unit S m⁻¹.

Types of Substances on the Basis of Conductivity

▶ Conductors : Substances which have large conductivity in the range of 10⁴ to 10⁷ S m⁻¹ are called conductors. These are intrinsic and extrinsic semiconductors.

▶ Insulators : Substances which have very low conductivity in the range of 10⁻²⁰ to 10⁻¹⁰ S m⁻¹ are called insulators. e.g., plastics, wood, coal, etc.

2.4 Types of Conductance

▶ Semiconductors : Substances which have moderate conductivity in the range of 10⁻⁶ to 10⁴ S m⁻¹ are called semiconductors. These are intrinsic and extrinsic semiconductors.

▶ Conductors : Substances which have large conductivity in the range of 10⁴ to 10⁷ S m⁻¹ are known as conductors e.g., metals and their alloys, certain non-metals like carbon black, graphite and some organic polymers.

▶ Superconductors : Materials which have zero resistivity and infinite conductivity are called superconductors e.g., metals and their alloys at very low temperature 0 to 15 K. Ceramic materials and mixed oxide behave as superconductors upto 150 K.

Types of Conductance & Factors Affecting Electronic Conductance

▶ Metallic or electronic conductance : Electrical conductance through metals due to movement of electrons is called electronic or metallic conductance e.g., all metals, alloys, graphite and conducting polymers.

Factors affecting electronic conductance :

  • The nature and structure of the metal.
  • The number of valence electrons per atom.
  • Temperature : electronic conductance decreases with increasing temperature.

Electrolytic or Ionic Conductance

▶ The conductance of electricity by ions present in the solution or melt is called ionic or electrolytic conductance. In ionic conductance, ions carry charge from one electrode to another.

Factors affecting ionic conductance :

  • The nature of the electrolyte added.
  • Size of the ions produced.
  • The nature and viscosity of solvent.
  • Solvation of the ions.
  • Concentration of electrolyte.
  • Temperature : electrolytic conductivity increases with increasing temperature because speed of ions increases.

Measurement of Conductivity of Ionic Solutions

▶ Conductance of an ionic solution is measured by measuring its resistance with the help of Wheatstone bridge.

First of all, cell constant of the conductivity cell is measured by measuring the resistance of solution whose conductivity is already known. The cell constant is calculated using formula, G* = κ A/R.

The conductivity cell is connected to one arm of the Wheatstone bridge containing two fixed and one variable resistance as shown. It is connected to an oscillator O and a detector P. The bridge is balanced when no current passes through the detector.

At equilibrium, R₁/R₂ = R₃/R₄ or R₄ = (R₃ · R₂)/R₁

Conductivity of the solution is calculated by using formula, κ = G*/R = (1/R) · (l/A) = G*/R

Molar conductance Λₘ is calculated by using formula, Λₘ = κ/M , where M is molarity of solution.

Variation of Conductivity and Molar Conductivity with Concentration

▶ Molar conductivity (Λₘ) is the conductance of a solution containing 1 mole of the electrolyte.

Λₘ = κ × 1000 / Molarity (where molarity is in mol L⁻¹)   Units for Λₘ = S m² mol⁻¹ or S cm² mol⁻¹.

2.4 Equivalent Conductivity (Λₑq)

▶ Equivalent conductivity (Λₑq) is the conductance of a solution containing 1 g equivalent of the electrolyte.

Λₑq = κ × 1000 / Normality (where, normality is in eq L⁻¹)   Units for Λₑq = S m² eq⁻¹ or S cm² eq⁻¹.

Variation of G, κ, Λₘ and Λₑq with dilution

  • On dilution, as no. of ions increases, conductance (G) increases.
  • On dilution, as no. of ions per cm³ decreases, κ decreases.
  • On dilution, though κ decreases but volume (V) increases much more. Hence, Λₘ or Λₑq increases because Λ = κ × V.

When concentration approaches zero i.e., at infinite dilution, the molar conductivity is known as limiting molar conductivity (Λ°ₘ).

Variation of Λₘ with concentration (C) :

▶ For strong electrolytes, there is only a small increase in conductance with dilution. This is because a strong electrolyte is completely dissociated in solution and so, the number of ions remains constant and on dilution, interionic attractions decreases as ions are far apart.

Λₘ increases slowly with dilution and can be represented by equation:

Λₘ = Λ°ₘ – A C¹ᐟ² (Debye — Hückel Onsager equation)

Graph of Λₘ vs √C for acetic acid (weak electrolyte) and potassium chloride (strong electrolyte)

Plot of Λₘ against C¹ᐟ² is a straight line with intercept equal to Λ°ₘ and slope equal to '–A'.

Thus, Λₘ decreases linearly with √C , when C = 0, Λₘ = Λ°ₘ and Λ°ₘ can be determined experimentally.

▶ For weak electrolytes, there is a very large increase in conductance with dilution especially near infinite dilution as no. of ions increases.

▶ Kohlrausch law of independent migration of ions : Λ°ₘ = ν₊λ°₊ + ν₋λ°₋ where λ°₊ and λ°₋ are the limiting molar conductivities of the cation and anion respectively and ν₊ and ν₋ are stoichiometric no. of cations and anions respectively in one formula unit of the electrolyte.

For example, limiting molar conductivity of sodium chloride is given as Λ°ₘ(NaCl) = λ°Na⁺ + λ°Cl⁻.

▶ For weak electrolyte, α = Λₘ / Λ°ₘ and Kₐ = (C α²) / (1 – α) = (C Λₘ²) / (Λ°ₘ (Λ°ₘ – Λₘ))

2.5 Electrolytic Cells and Electrolysis

▶ Electrolysis is the process of decomposition of an electrolyte by passing electricity through its aqueous solution or molten state.

▶ Faraday's first law of electrolysis : The amount of chemical reaction which occurs at any electrode during electrolysis by a current is proportional to the quantity of electricity passed through the electrolyte (solution or melt).

W ∝ Q or W = Z Q = Z × I × t ; where Z is electrochemical equivalent of the substance deposited and Z = Eq. wt. of substance / 96500

▶ Faraday's second law of electrolysis : The amounts of different substances liberated by the same quantity of electricity passing through the electrolytic solution are proportional to their chemical equivalent weights.

W₁/W₂ = E₁/E₂ , where E is the equivalent weight = Atomic mass / No. of electrons gained or lost / oxidation / reduction of the element

2.8 Corrosion (HD)

▶ Corrosion : The slow eating away of metals when exposed to the atmosphere is called corrosion.

▶ Corrosion of iron (Rusting) : It is an electrochemical phenomenon which occurs in the presence of moisture and oxygen.

At anode: 2Fe(s) → 2Fe²⁺(aq) + 4e⁻ ; E°(Fe²⁺/Fe) = –0.44 V

At cathode: O₂(g) + 4H⁺(aq) + 4e⁻ → 2H₂O(l) ; E°(H⁺/O₂/H₂O) = +1.23 V

Overall reaction : 2Fe(s) + O₂(g) + 4H⁺(aq) → 2Fe²⁺(aq) + 2H₂O(l) ; E°cell = 1.67 V

▶ Methods used for prevention of corrosion are barrier protection, sacrificial protection and anti-rust solutions.

2.9 Hydrogen Economy

▶ Hydrogen can be used as renewable and non-polluting source of energy. Water will split into hydrogen and oxygen using solar energy.

H₂O(l) → H₂(g) + ½ O₂(g) (using solar energy)

▶ Combustion of hydrogen in a fuel cell will produce water.

H₂(g) + ½ O₂(g) → H₂O(l)

▶ Both these technologies are based on electrochemical principles. This production of energy will be very economical in comparison to fossil fuels and nuclear energy.
